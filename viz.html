Interactive Visualisations

Homework 5 — three interactive visualizations on the CMA internal migration panel

This page presents three interactive Plotly visualisations of the merged CMA panel at ../data/processed/panel_cma.csv (40 CMAs × 24 years = 960 observations, 2001–2024). Each view answers a different question about the internal migration patterns.

Figure 1 — Net intraprovincial migration rate across Canada, 2001–2024

This map places each of the 40 CMAs at its city-centre coordinates; bubble size is population, bubble colour is net intraprovincial migration rate (migrants per 1,000 residents, net of outflows). The year slider below the map controls which year’s data is shown and provides the option to show animation over 2001-2024. Hover over any bubble to see the CMA name, year, population, and migration rates.

Figure 1. Net intraprovincial migration rate per 1,000 population at each CMA, animated across 2001–2024.

Toronto, Montréal, and Vancouver begin turning red around 2015–2016, suggesting that intraprovincial out-migration from large CMAs was already underway as a longer-term structural trend. The Covid window (2020–2024) deepens the colour for these cities noticeably, but whether the pandemic represents a genuine structural shift or an acceleration of an existing trend needs to be assessed more rigorously. It motivates the formal statistical inference using OLS in the written report. Cities like Barrie, Kelowna, and Sherbrooke appear increasingly blue as time progresses. Although this project does not model bilateral flows directly, the simultaneous pattern of large CMAs losing residents while nearby small and medium CMAs gain strongly suggests that the small to medium CMAs adjacent to Toronto, Vancouver, and Montréal are the primary destinations of the out-migrating residents.

Figure 2 — Interactive per-CMA migration time series

The dropdown menu controls which CMA is displayed. The chart then shows that CMA’s net migration rates from 2001 to 2024 as two lines: blue for interprovincial and red for intraprovincial. The shaded band highlights the Covid/post-Covid window between 2020–2024.

Figure 2. Per-CMA net migration rate (blue line: interprovincial, red line: intraprovincial) with a CMA dropdown. The orange band marks 2020–2024.

The blue (interprovincial) and red (intraprovincial) lines often tell different stories for the same city. For most CMAs, the red line shows similar or greater variation over time than the blue line. For instance, Toronto’s red line drops sharply in the Covid window (2020–2022) while its blue line is comparatively flat. However, exceptions exist: in Edmonton, Halifax, and Regina the red line is relatively stable across the panel while the blue (interprovincial) line carries most of the temporal change, suggesting that movement across provinces accounts for most of the migration variability in those cities. This visualization allows us to verify whether any individual CMA’s trajectory matches the aggregate patterns noted in the written report.

Figure 3 — Provincial unemployment rate vs. net interprovincial migration rate, animated by year

Each bubble corresponds to one CMA in one year: x-axis is the provincial unemployment rate (all CMAs in the same province share the same x-value), y-axis is net interprovincial migration rate per 1,000, bubble size is population, and CMAs in the same province share a colour. The year slider controls which year’s cross sectional data is shown and provides the option to animate across 2001–2024. Hover over any bubble to see the CMA name, year, unemployment rate, interprovincial rate, and population.

Figure 3. Provincial unemployment rate vs. net interprovincial migration rate per 1,000, bubble size = population, colour = province, animated across 2001–2024.

Employment opportunities as a pull factor for interprovincial migration is visible in the Alberta cluster (red). In the early 2000s and again around 2014, Alberta’s low unemployment coincides with positive interprovincial rates, so the province was attracting workers from provinces with higher unemployment rates. Following the oil price collapse in around 2015, Alberta’s unemployment rises and its interprovincial migration rate turns negative, as it begins losing residents to other provinces. Ontario and British Columbia CMAs (blue and green) cluster near zero on the y-axis throughout, reflecting their relatively stable labour markets and large population bases. Atlantic CMAs (Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Newfoundland) sit at the higher end of the x-axis for most years, with negative or near-zero net interprovincial migration rates. Before 2020, each annual cross-section scatter plot shows a weak to moderate negative association between unemployment rate and net interprovincial rate. That is, provinces with lower unemployment tend to attract more interprovincial migrants. This pattern largely disappears in 2020, when all provinces’ unemployment rates spiked simultaneously due to Covid-19, disrupting the normal migration dynamics.
